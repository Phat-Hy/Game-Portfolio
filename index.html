81 Days and Nights (2026)

Enemy AI & gameplay systems

A historical single-player FPS set during the 1972 Battle of Quang Tri, with a four-mission campaign and an endless Survival mode. I was the engineer behind the enemy AI — the part that decides whether a squad of soldiers reads as coordinated, or as twelve copies of the same soldier.

Contributions

  • NavMesh-driven AI with three behaviour modes (Wander / Ambush / Sniper) and line-of-sight perception
  • Cover system with positional claims, so squadmates stop stacking on the same wall
  • Peek and flanking behaviours, with engagement distances spread across the squad
  • Editor tools: spawner inspectors, a CLI verification harness, an automated API integration runner
  • Performance work: path recalculation throttled to 2 Hz, limit on concurrently active agents

AI Balancing Service (2026)

Backend & live operations

Tuning enemy difficulty by hand does not survive real players. This is the service behind 81 Days and Nights: it collects what happened in each fight, scores every enemy configuration, and breeds the next generation of parameters — separately for every player.

Contributions

  • Genetic algorithm: four candidates per generation, elitist selection, 60/40 blending crossover, ±5% mutation
  • Fitness scored from combat telemetry, with mutation scaled by the player's death rate
  • REST API with JWT-secured admin endpoints and SHA-256 hashed credentials
  • Admin dashboard for live combat logs, generation tracking and manual overrides
  • CI/CD through GitHub Actions to Azure App Service

About

I'm a Unity developer from Vietnam. I studied Software Engineering at FPT University — an AQAS-accredited programme built on project-based learning, where every semester ends in something you can actually run rather than an exam.

That suits how I like to work. My capstone grew into 81 Days and Nights, a Steam-bound FPS, and the enemy AI I wrote for it is the work I'm proudest of: the AI that looks smart in isolation and falls apart the moment twelve of them exist at once, the cover logic that only breaks once a whole squad is competing for it. The balancing service came out of the same frustration — difficulty tuned by hand stops scaling the moment real players are involved.

Outside of that I keep building small things to learn mechanics properly. The fastest way to understand why a game feels the way it does is to rebuild the part that makes it feel that way.
